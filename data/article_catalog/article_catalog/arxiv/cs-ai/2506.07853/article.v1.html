A Temporal FRBR/FRBRoo-Based Model for
Component-Level Versioning of Legal Norms

Hudson de Martim Federal Senate of Brazil
hudsonm@senado.leg.br
Abstract

Effectively representing legal norms for automated processing is a critical challenge, particularly in tracking the diachronic evolution of their hierarchical components (e.g., articles, paragraphs). While foundational frameworks like FRBR/FRBRoo and standards like Akoma Ntoso model legal documents at a macro level, they lack native mechanisms for granular, component-level versioning. This limitation hinders the deterministic point-in-time reconstruction of legal texts, a fundamental capability for reliable Legal Tech and AI applications. This paper proposes a structured, temporal model that extends the FRBRoo framework to address this gap. It introduces specialized subclasses of Expression—Temporal Version (TV) and Language Version (LV)—to represent the state of a legal norm and its linguistic variations at specific points in time. The model applies this same paradigm hierarchically, introducing Component Work (CW), Component Temporal Version (CTV), and Component Language Version (CLV) to track the lifecycle of individual articles, paragraphs, and clauses. Using the Brazilian Federal Constitution as a case study, the paper demonstrates how each amendment creates new Component Temporal Versions for affected provisions, while unaffected components retain their existing versions. This fine-grained, time-aware architecture enables the precise, deterministic retrieval and reconstruction of any part of a legal text as it existed on a specific date. The model provides a robust foundation for developing advanced legal information systems, knowledge graphs, and AI tools capable of accurate historical analysis and impact assessment, overcoming the limitations of current generative models.

Keywords: FRBRoo; Temporal Modeling; Legal Knowledge Graph; Legal Text Evolution; Component-Level Versioning; Akoma Ntoso; LexML.

1 Introduction

The increasing demand for transparency, efficiency in legal research, and the proliferation of Legal Tech and Artificial Intelligence (AI) applications underscore the critical need for machine-readable representations of legal norms [1]. Effectively representing these norms for automated processing, however, requires a nuanced approach that captures not only the entirety of a legal instrument but also its constituent parts and, crucially, its dynamic state over time [2] [3].

While foundational conceptual frameworks—such as the Functional Requirements for Bibliographic Records (FRBR/FRBRoo) and the XML schema standard Akoma Ntoso—provide robust approaches for representing legal documents and their macro-level evolution [4], they typically focus on entire instruments (Works/Expressions/Manifestations) rather than on sub-documentary elements. Although the OASIS LegalDocML initiative and related European modeling efforts (e.g., OntoLegis) have shown how to encode legislative texts in XML, these initiatives do not fully address the need to track temporally discrete, component-level changes (titles, articles, paragraphs) with explicit versioning metadata.

This challenge is particularly acute when considering the capabilities of current Generative AI. For instance, Brazil’s 1988 Federal Constitution has been amended over one hundred times since its enactment. Each amendment may insert, delete, or modify specific articles and paragraphs.Today’s large language models (LLMs) and other generative AI tools operate on a probabilistic basis, which, despite their substantial capabilities, makes them inherently unsuitable for tasks requiring absolute precision. They lack deterministic mechanisms to retrieve or reconstruct the exact text of the Constitution as it stood on a particular past date (e.g., January 10, 2005), frequently leading to inadvertent omissions or discrepancies. This limitation highlights a critical gap, as the high-stakes nature of the legal field demands verifiable accuracy and deterministic reliability, where correctness is not a probability but a requirement.

Against this backdrop, the present paper proposes and details a specialized adaptation of the FRBRoo conceptual model—augmented by Akoma Ntoso-inspired metadata conventions—to deliver a fully structured, semantically-rich representation of Brazilian legal norms and their diachronic evolution. We demonstrate how FRBRoo’s core concepts (Work, Expression, Manifestation) can be extended into a multi-tier hierarchy that captures not only the normative instrument as a whole but also its internal elements (titles, chapters, articles, paragraphs) and each of those elements’ variants across time and language. Crucially, this paper advances the FRBRoo model by introducing two complementary refinements: Temporal Version, which is a subclass of Expression specifically designed to capture the exact textual state of a norm (or its components) on a given date, and Language Version, which encapsulates the linguistic variants of a given expression in different official languages.

By instantiating a Temporal Version for each amendment event—and linking that version deterministically to the relevant Work and Manifestation—we achieve a precise, date-specific representation of the legal norm (and its components). Similarly, Language Version objects allow the system to maintain parallel translations of each Temporal Version. This fine-grained, time-aware architecture forms the basis for building deterministic pipelines capable of reconstructing compiled (unofficial consolidated) texts as they existed at any point in history, thus overcoming present limitations of generative AI models. By doing so, our model establishes the verifiable factual ground truth required for advanced computational legal reasoning systems to operate reliably.

This paper is structured as follows. Section 2 reviews foundational concepts from the FRBRoo model and examines related legal information frameworks, including LexML Brazil and Akoma Ntoso, highlighting their treatment of norm identity, structure, and temporality. Section 3 introduces our core proposal: a structured, temporally-aware model for representing legal norms grounded in the Work–Expression–Manifestation (WEM) framework. Section 4 details how this model is extended to represent the internal hierarchical components of norms (e.g., articles, paragraphs, clauses), introducing the notions of Component Work (CW), Component Temporal Version (CTV), and Component Language Version (CLV) and discusses the temporal evolution and multilingual expression of components, using the caput of Article 6 of the Brazilian Federal Constitution as a detailed example. Section 5 concludes the paper and outlines future research directions, including formal ontology development, knowledge graph implementation, and integration with AI and NLP applications.

2 Fundamental Concepts

To ground our proposal in established theory, this section reviews how existing frameworks—namely LexML Brazil and Akoma Ntoso—leverage the FRBRoo concepts of Work, Expression.

2.1 FRBRoo Overview

The Functional Requirements for Bibliographic Records (FRBRoo) model, originally developed by the International Federation of Library Associations and Institutions (IFLA) for bibliographic cataloging [5], provides a conceptual framework for understanding information resources. FRBRoo, an object-oriented version harmonized with the CIDOC Conceptual Reference Model (CIDOC CRM), extends this framework into a formal ontology, enhancing its applicability across diverse domains, including the legal field.

The core of the FRBRoo model lies in its clear distinction between the abstract intellectual or artistic content and its various physical and representational forms. It defines three main entities:

  • •

    a Work (FRBRoo F1), representing a distinct abstract intellectual creation. In the legal domain, this corresponds to the normative intent or conceptual scope of a law, independent of any particular textual instantiation.

  • •

    an Expression (FRBRoo F2), representing a specific realization of a Work. This includes, for example, the text of a statute in a given language or version. Through CIDOC CRM’s E52 Time-Span, FRBRoo can record validity periods for each Expression, enabling temporal annotations.

  • •

    a Manifestation (FRBRoo F3 or F4), representing the physical or digital carrier of an Expression. In practice, this may be an official gazette publication, or any other distribution format.

Additionally, an Item (FRBRoo F5) represents a single exemplar of a Manifestation, such as a specific printed copy.

This tripartite distinction is particularly powerful in the legal domain. The abstract normative intent of a legal norm (Work) is distinct from its specific authoritative textual formulation in a given language (Expression), and is again distinct from its various published formats (Manifestation). By leveraging CIDOC CRM’s temporal constructs, each Expression can be associated with explicit start and end dates, thus capturing its valid time-span. Such temporal annotation is essential for tracking amendments, translations, and the evolution of legal texts over time.

The following subsections discuss how established legal information initiatives, Akoma Ntoso and LexML Brazil, have adopted and operationalized these FRBRoo concepts, including their strategies (or gaps) in temporal modeling.

2.2 Akoma Ntoso Approach

Akoma Ntoso (AKN) [6] is an XML vocabulary specifically designed for representing parliamentary, legislative, judicial, and soft law documents in a machine-readable format. Its conceptual design is aligned with FRBRoo, providing a structured framework for legal documents at different levels of abstraction:

  • •

    Work: In AKN, the abstract legal content (the Work) is identified through unique identifiers (e.g., the <FRBRWork> element). This allows consistent references to the underlying legal concept, independently of text variants or publication forms.

  • •

    Expression: AKN models the specific authoritative text (the Expression) within elements such as <FRBRExpression>. It separates text content from metadata, enabling detailed semantic annotations (e.g., through <paragraph>, <article>, and semantic tag sets). Multilingual support is provided via separate <FRBRExpression> entries for each language version.

  • •

    Manifestation: The physical or digital carrier of an Expression (the Manifestation) is represented by elements like <FRBRManifestation>, which capture details such as format (XML, HTML, PDF) and publication metadata (e.g., <publicationDate>, <version>).

Although AKN provides mechanisms to record publication dates and version identifiers at the document level, it does not natively support fine-grained temporal annotations for individual components (such as articles or paragraphs). In practice, one can include timestamps via custom attributes or extended metadata, but AKN’s core schema lacks built-in constructs to track, for example, when a specific article was amended or replaced. Consequently, while AKN excels at structural and semantic tagging, it requires additional conventions or extensions to capture the component-level, time-specific versions that our proposal will formalize.

This reveals a fundamental distinction in modeling approaches. While Akoma Ntoso, for example, provides mechanisms to record temporal metadata, it largely leaves the interpretation and application of complex temporal logic—especially at a granular, component level—to external processes or systems.

In contrast, our proposed model explicitly reifies these temporal states as distinct ontological entities (namely, as subclasses of F22 SelfContainedExpression). This design choice makes the temporal evolution and relationships of legal texts and their components explicit within the ontology itself, rather than depending on the implicit interpretation of metadata attributes. This distinction is critical for AI applications. Implicit temporal data requires complex, often heuristic-based reasoning engines to infer the correct textual state at a given point in time. By modeling each temporal state as an explicit entity, however, our approach drastically simplifies deterministic reconstruction, querying, and reasoning. The strength of our proposal thus lies in its commitment to making these implicit temporal dynamics explicit and formally structured.

2.3 LexML Brazil Approach

The LexML Brazil project111LexML Brazil, Legislative Integration Project. Available at: https://projeto.lexml.gov.br. Accessed on: 08 Jun 2025. explicitly adopts the FRBRoo as its foundational reference for legislative and legal information. FRBRoo enables the representation of both the creation process of a work—captured as a Work and its Expression embodied in an original (Singleton Manifestation)—and the subsequent reproduction process that yields multiple copies (Items) of that original.

According to the LexML Reference Model [7], legislative and legal information can be perceived at three levels of abstraction:

  • •

    Physical or Digital Object (Manifestation): for example, the original document signed by the President of the Republic (the autograph or <F4 Manifestation>) or the PDF published by the Official Gazette (<F3 Manifestation Product Type>).

  • •

    Linguistic Object (Expression): the textual content conveyed by these media (e.g., <F2 Expression> in a particular language).

  • •

    Semantic Object (Work): the normative content or legal intent conveyed in the text (e.g., <F1 Work> representing the abstract legal norm).

LexML’s core tenet is that a legal norm (Work) is created at the moment of its signature by the competent authority, and its publication is the mechanism through which it enters into force. To support this lifecycle, LexML uses the following temporal metadata:

  • •

    <dataRepresentativa> (representative date): the date of signature, which identifies an instance of an <F15 ComplexWork> corresponding to the new norm.

  • •

    <dataVersao> (effective date): the date when a specific version of the norm becomes valid.

  • •

    <dataVisao> (versioning event date): the date of an event that generates a new version or variant of the norm (e.g., rectification, repeal, or declaration of unconstitutionality).

These temporal attributes allow LexML to identify and distinguish different document-level versions and variants over time. However, LexML does not natively provide explicit temporal annotations for individual components (such as an article or paragraph). While it is possible to include custom attributes or extended metadata within the XML schema, the core model applies temporal markers at the norm level rather than at finer granularity. Consequently, LexML ensures that the abstract norm, its authoritative text, and its public embodiments remain distinct yet interconnected, but it leaves a gap in tracking component-level changes—precisely the gap our proposed model aims to fill.

It is worth noting that the LexML URN standard, with its norm@version~language!part syntax, provides an advanced and powerful identification scheme for temporal components. This syntax correctly acknowledges the need to uniquely address a specific part of a norm as it existed on a given date. However, an identification scheme is not a representation model in itself. The URN acts as a pointer to a resource but does not formally define the relationships between resources. For instance, it does not explicitly model that !art6_cpt@2000-02-14 supersedes a previous version or that both are temporal expressions of the same abstract Component Work. Our proposed FRBRoo-based model provides precisely this missing semantic layer: the formal, explicit representation of entities and their diachronic relationships, thereby supplying the necessary ontological foundation to fully leverage such a powerful naming convention in a deterministic system.

It is important to situate our contribution within the broader academic landscape of temporal versioning for legal knowledge. Research into temporal ontology versioning [8] [9] addresses the critical challenge of managing the evolution of the ontology itself—that is, the changes in abstract concepts, properties, and logical rules over time. Our model addresses a distinct yet complementary problem. We focus specifically on versioning the textual realization of legal norms (the FRBRoo F22 Expression) and their hierarchical components. We are providing the stable, versioned textual ground truth that is a necessary prerequisite for any subsequent semantic modeling or logical reasoning. Clarifying this focus highlights our unique contribution and avoids perceived redundancy with works centered on conceptual-level versioning.

3 FRBRoo-Based Representation of Legal Norms

The FRBRoo model, originally developed for library cataloging, has proven highly adaptable for describing complex information resources across various domains, including legal information. In this section, we detail how FRBRoo’s core entities—Work, Expression, and Manifestation—map to the lifecycle of a legal norm, preparing the ground for our subsequent introduction of temporal and language refinements.

When applied to legal norms, this tripartite distinction is instantiated as follows:

  • •

    Work. The legal norm represents the abstract intellectual content or fundamental legal idea established through the legislative process. It is the “what” of the legal norm, independent of any textual formulation or medium. In practice, the Work is formalized at the moment of its signature (i.e., its official promulgation) by the competent authority, marking the conclusion of the legislative process.222It is important to clarify that this model represents the product of the legislative process—the final, enacted norm and its subsequent temporal states—rather than modeling the legislative procedure itself (e.g., bill drafting, debates, or voting). This aligns with the LexML Brazil project’s approach, which identifies the Work using a specific subclass of <F1 Work>—namely, <F15 ComplexWork>333In FRBRoo, an F15 ComplexWork represents a bibliographic Work that is composed of multiple intellectual Works, such as compilations or consolidated collections of laws.—to capture its aggregate nature. The identification is made via the <dataRepresentativa> element (date of signature).

  • •

    Expression. An Expression is the specific linguistic and intellectual realization of a Work. For a legal norm, this corresponds to the authoritative textual formulation as drafted and signed by the competent authority at the culmination of the legislative process. LexML models the original signed document as an <F4 ManifestationSingleton>444In FRBRoo, an F4 ManifestationSingleton represents a unique physical object that embodies a single Expression, such as an original manuscript or signed decree. containing an <F22 SelfContainedExpression>555In FRBRoo, an F22 SelfContainedExpression represents a complete, distinct realization of a Work’s content., which holds the normative text. Distinct linguistic versions (for example, an English translation of a Portuguese statute) are modeled as separate Expressions of the same Work.

  • •

    Manifestation. A Manifestation is the physical or digital carrier of an Expression. In the legal domain, this refers primarily to the official publication venue—commonly the Official Gazette—where the norm is made publicly accessible and thereby acquires legal force. Different file formats or publication media (for example, an HTML page versus a PDF rendition of the same gazette issue) are considered distinct Manifestations. An Item (modeled as <F5 Item> in FRBRoo) is a single exemplar of a Manifestation, such as one printed copy of the Official Gazette or one downloaded PDF file.

By clearly distinguishing these three levels, we can model a legal norm’s lifecycle from its abstract conception (Work) through its authoritative text (Expression) to its public dissemination (Manifestation), while also accounting for multiple language renditions and publication formats. In Section 4, we will extend this base model by introducing Temporal Version and Language Version refinements, which enable fine-grained tracking of component-level changes (e.g., individual articles or paragraphs) over time and across languages.

3.1 Practical Mapping of Work, Expression, and Manifestation

The FRBRoo model defines an Expression as a specific intellectual realization of a Work. In particular, FRBRoo’s <F22 SelfContainedExpression> class represents a complete, standalone textual realization of a Work. Building on this, we introduce two specialized subclasses of <F22 SelfContained Expression>, which is a subclass of <F2 Expression>:

  • •

    Temporal Version (TV). A subclass of <F22 SelfContainedExpression> that captures the exact textual state of a legal norm on a specific date or event. In other words, each TV is a dated Expression of the Work. Formally:

    TemporalVersion⊑F22 SelfContainedExpressionsquare-image-of-or-equalsTemporalVersionF22 SelfContainedExpression\texttt{TemporalVersion}\;\sqsubseteq\;\texttt{F22 SelfContainedExpression}TemporalVersion ⊑ F22 SelfContainedExpression

    where TemporalVersion includes mandatory properties such as validFromDate and (optionally) validToDate to record its precise time span.

  • •

    Language Version (LV). A subclass of F22 SelfContainedExpression that represents a monolingual textual realization derived from a given Temporal Version. Each LV is therefore a language-specific Expression of the same Work. Formally:

    LanguageVersion⊑F22 SelfContainedExpressionsquare-image-of-or-equalsLanguageVersionF22 SelfContainedExpression\texttt{LanguageVersion}\;\sqsubseteq\;\texttt{F22 SelfContainedExpression}LanguageVersion ⊑ F22 SelfContainedExpression

    and is linked to exactly one Temporal Version via a property such as derivesFromTemporal Version.

In FRBRoo notation:

  • •

    Each TemporalVersion is an instance of <F22 SelfContainedExpression> enriched with temporal properties (e.g., E52 Time-Span from CIDOC CRM).

  • •

    Each LanguageVersion is an instance of <F22 SelfContainedExpression> extended by a language attribute (ISO 639-1 code) and a derivesFromTemporalVersion relationship.

Figure 1 illustrates how a single Work (the abstract norm) may have multiple Temporal Versions (each a dated <F22 SelfContainedExpression>), and how each Temporal Version may have one or more Language Versions (also F22 instances with a specific language).

By defining Temporal Version and Language Version as explicit subclasses of <F22 SelfContained Expression>, we maintain strict alignment with FRBRoo while clarifying that each “version” is, in fact, a specialized kind of Expression.

In jurisdictions with multiple official languages—such as Canada or the European Union—a legal norm’s Temporal Version is inherently multilingual, comprising one distinct Language Version for each official language.

In contrast, in monolingual jurisdictions (including Brazil), each Temporal Version is effectively monolingual, so the single Language Version coincides with the Temporal Version (see Figure 2).

The Signed Document of the original version—whether a physical paper with a handwritten signature or a digitally signed authoritative file—depicted in Figure 2 is the first and most authoritative Manifestation of that version. In FRBRoo terms, this is an <F4 ManifestationSingleton> if it is a unique physical object.

The Official Publication of the original version (e.g., in the Official Gazette) is a subsequent Manifestation, intended for public dissemination and to confer broader legal effect or notice. It reproduces the content of the original Expression, typically as an <F3 ManifestationProductType> when mass-produced (e.g., multiple copies of the gazette issue).

By distinguishing Temporal Versions (as Expressions) and their derived Language Versions, and by linking each LV to its respective Manifestation, we establish a clear, deterministic mapping that supports precise point-in-time retrieval and accurate consolidation of legal texts.

3.2 Legal Norm Compilation

In Brazil, a Compilation (“Compilação” in Portuguese) is the process by which, starting from a specified Temporal Version (TV0) of a legal norm—such as the original text—all modifications introduced by subsequent amending acts up to a target date D𝐷Ditalic_D are incorporated, thereby producing an (unofficial) consolidated text, denoted as TVn at date D𝐷Ditalic_D.

As defined previously, each Temporal Version (TV) captures the precise textual state of a norm at a particular point in time. When a new legislative act amends an existing norm, it always applies to the most recent Temporal Version of that norm, yielding a new, subsequent TV. For example, consider the Brazilian Federal Constitution of 1988:

  • •

    1988-10-05: TV0, the inaugural Temporal Version corresponding to the original promulgation.

  • •

    1992-03-31: TV1, resulting from Constitutional Amendment No. 1 of 1992-03-31, which modified TV0.

  • •

    1993-03-17: TV2, created when Constitutional Amendment No. 3 of 1993-03-17 amended TV1.

  • •

    1993-09-14: TV3, due to Constitutional Amendment No. 4 of 1993-09-14 amending TV2.

Note that Constitutional Amendment No. 2 of 1992-08-25 did not alter the text of the Constitution and therefore did not generate a new Temporal Version of the primary norm.

To generate a compiled Temporal Version TVn of a norm at a given date D𝐷Ditalic_D, where n>0𝑛0n>0italic_n > 0, the compilation process requires:

  1. 1.

    The immediately preceding compiled Temporal Version, TVn-1, of the same norm.

  2. 2.

    The original Temporal Versions (TV0) of any amending norms that took effect on D𝐷Ditalic_D and whose modifications target TVn-1.

Those amending acts (each TV0 of an amendment) are applied to TVn-1 to produce TVn, which represents the consolidated (editorial) text of the norm on date D𝐷Ditalic_D. In FRBRoo terms, TVn would be modeled as a new instance of TemporalVersion with a consolidates property listing TVn-1 and the relevant TV0 instances of the amending acts as its sources.

Figure 3 illustrates this process: TVn is linked to TVn-1 and to all TV0 of amending acts via the consolidates property, enabling deterministic reconstruction of the compiled (unofficial consolidated) text on any target date.

By formalizing Compilation in this way, we ensure that every compiled (unofficial consolidated) Temporal Version can be precisely traced back to its immediate predecessor and all relevant amending acts, thereby supporting accurate, point-in-time retrieval and editorial consolidation of legal norms.

4 Legal Norm’s Components Representation

Legal norms are not monolithic entities; they possess an internal hierarchical structure. This structure is composed of various Components, which are identifiable, addressable subdivisions of the legal text below the level of the norm as a whole. These components serve to organize the normative content and often convey distinct legal propositions. Common examples include titles, chapters, sections, articles, “caput,” paragraphs, items, and clauses (or subitems). Just as the overarching legal norm evolves and can be expressed in multiple languages, so too can its individual components.

The challenge, therefore, is to represent not only the norm in its entirety but also each of its constituent parts in a way that captures their individual semantic content, their linguistic variations, and their temporal evolution. This granular approach is essential for precise legal analysis, accurate information retrieval, and enabling sophisticated AI applications that can reason about specific parts of a law over time.

4.1 Applying the FRBRoo Model to Components

Mirroring the representation of the legal norm as a whole (Section 3), each individual Component can also be conceptualized through the FRBRoo lens of Work, Expression, and Manifestation. This ensures a uniform modeling paradigm across all levels of a legal text’s hierarchy.

  • •

    Component Work (CW). Represents the abstract normative content of a specific component (e.g., the legal rule conveyed by an article’s caput, or the scope defined by a chapter’s title). It is the “what” of that textual subdivision, independent of wording or language. Formally, each CW corresponds to a FRBRoo <F1 Work> at the component level and persists even when its Expression changes due to amendments.

  • •

    Component Expression. A specific realization of a Component Work. We introduce two specialized subclasses:

    • –

      Component Temporal Version (CTV). A subclass of FRBRoo <F22 SelfContained Expression> that captures the exact textual state of a Component Work at a given point in time. The first CTV corresponds to the component’s original text as enacted. Each amendment affecting that component yields a new CTV, each annotated with a validFromDate (and optionally validToDate), recording its effective period.

    • –

      Component Language Version (CLV). A subclass of FRBRoo <F22 SelfContained Expression> that represents a monolingual realization of a specific CTV. Each CLV is linked to exactly one CTV (via a property such as derivesFromComponentTemporalVersion) and carries a language attribute (ISO 639-1 code). For example, the original Portuguese text of CTV0 is a CLV; if an English translation is later created, that is a separate CLV of the same CTV.

  • •

    Component Manifestation (CM). A physical or digital embodiment of a CLV. Although uncommon in practice (since components usually appear embedded in a larger document), CM can be modeled as instances of FRBRoo <F3 ManifestationProductType> (for mass-distributed formats) or <F4 ManifestationSingleton> (for unique artifacts). For example, a standalone PDF file containing only the text of Title I would be a CM of the corresponding CLV.

By treating each component as a mini-Work with its own temporal and language Expressions (CTV and CLV), and optional Manifestations, we achieve full granularity. Each CW has a lifecycle: multiple CTVs (each dated), each spawning one or more CLVs (each language-specific), which may in turn be linked to individual CMs if needed. This detailed structure enables precise, component-level tracking of amendments, multilingual versions, and publication formats.

4.2 Temporal Evolution and Multilingualism of Components

The critical insight here is that legal amendments often target specific components within a norm, rather than the entire document. When an amending norm modifies, adds, or revokes a component (e.g., changes the wording of a paragraph, adds a new item to an article, or repeals an entire section), it effectively creates a new Component Temporal Version (CTV) for that specific component. The Component Work associated with that structural position persists (e.g., the concept of “Article X” remains), but its textual realization changes. Components not affected by the amendment retain their existing CTVs.

This component-level versioning is essential. It allows us to precisely track what changed, when it changed, and what the exact text of any part of a norm was on any given date. Similarly, if a component’s text, as defined by a specific CTV, is translated into another language, this creates a new Component Language Version (CLV) linked to that CTV.

Notably, the consolidates property, introduced in Section 3.2 to describe the compilation of a norm as a whole, applies with the same logic at this granular level. When an amendment modifies a specific component, the newly created Component Temporal Version (CTV) can be formally modeled as compiling (consolidating) two sources:

  1. 1.

    Its immediate predecessor CTV (the text before the change).

  2. 2.

    The CTV 0 (original) of the specific modifying component from the amending legal norm’s (the text that dictates the change).

This ensures a consistent, recursive modeling pattern across all hierarchical levels of the legal text, reinforcing the model’s structural robustness and analytical power for tracing changes with precision.

4.2.1 Practical Example: Caput of Article 6 of the Brazilian Federal Constitution of 1988

Consider the caput (main provision) of Article 6 of the Brazilian Federal Constitution of 1988, which enumerates social rights. 5 Conclusion and Future Work

This paper has proposed and detailed a structured, temporal model for representing legal norms and their hierarchical components, grounded in the FRBRoo concepts of Work, Expression, and Manifestation. By extending these principles from the entire norm down to its individual articles, paragraphs, and other subdivisions—introducing the notions of Temporal Versions (TV/CTV) and Language Versions (LV/CLV) at both levels—our proposal offers several key advantages:

  • •

    Precision in Temporal Tracking: Enables the exact history of each textual part of a norm to be recorded and retrieved, facilitating precise determination of its wording on any given date.

  • •

    Targeted Impact Analysis: Facilitates accurate mapping of an amendment’s impact to the specific components it affects, producing new Component Temporal Versions (CTVs) only for those components.

  • •

    Enhanced Information Retrieval: Supports queries targeted at individual components and their historical versions, improving the accuracy and relevance of search results within complex legal corpora.

  • •

    Deterministic Reconstruction: In direct contrast to the probabilistic and approximative nature of generative AI, our model enables the unambiguous, bit-by-bit reconstruction of legal texts for any point in time. This focus on determinism provides the reliability and verifiability that are primordial for high-stakes legal applications, establishing a foundation of trust that is unattainable with general-purpose AI models.

  • •

    Foundation for Advanced AI: Provides a structured, granular, and temporally-aware data model essential for AI systems to deterministically generate point-in-time compiled (unofficial consolidated) texts, compare provisions across versions, and understand the semantic evolution of legal concepts.

The proposed model—illustrated with examples from Brazilian legislation—establishes a robust foundation for next-generation legal information systems and addresses current limitations in AI’s handling of dynamic, component-level legal texts.

Future work will focus on four main areas:

  • •

    Formal Ontological Development. Create a comprehensive ontology that formally specifies all entities, subclasses, and relationships introduced in this model, aligning with existing standards such as LKIF-Core or LegalDocML.

  • •

    Integration of Event-Centric Modeling. Our model focuses on representing the versioned textual outcomes of legislative changes. A powerful future enhancement would be to explicitly model the legislative events that trigger these version transitions. Drawing inspiration from event-centric ontologies like the one proposed by Lima et al. [4] and the core E5 Event in CIDOC CRM, one could model an "Amendment Event" as a distinct entity. This would allow for capturing richer contextual information about the change itself, such as the cause and type of a change directly to its effect on the textual components.

  • •

    Formal Comparative Analysis. Conduct a formal analysis comparing the expressivity, computational tractability, and accuracy of inferring temporal states from implicit Akoma Ntoso metadata versus directly leveraging the explicit ontological entities proposed in this model. Such a study would provide strong empirical validation for the design choices presented in this paper.

  • •

    Knowledge Graph Implementation and Tooling. The proposed FRBRoo model is not merely conceptual; it is designed to serve as the direct ontological schema for a large-scale, temporally-aware legal knowledge graph. The entities defined (Work, Expression, TV, CTV, etc.) and their relationships (consolidates, derivesFromTemporalVersion) map directly to the nodes and edges of such a graph. A primary direction for future work is therefore the practical implementation and population of this knowledge graph with historical legislation. This will involve developing automated parsing pipelines, component identification algorithms, and procedures for instantiating and linking the versioned entities, ultimately enabling a rigorous evaluation of the model’s utility for tasks like point-in-time text reconstruction and amendment impact tracing.

  • •

    Advanced Reasoning Capabilities. Build upon the structured knowledge graph [10] to develop reasoning mechanisms—such as automated detection of normative conflicts or redundancies introduced by new amendments at the component level, and tracing the semantic drift of specific legal terms or concepts across components over time.

  • •

    Rigorous Benchmarking for Deterministic Accuracy. A critical future step is to develop evaluation benchmarks to rigorously test the model’s primary success metric: its ability to perform flawless, deterministic point-in-time reconstruction of legal corpora. This will empirically validate the model’s design philosophy, which consciously prioritizes verifiability and accuracy over other potential metrics, reflecting the non-negotiable demand for precision in the legal domain.

  • •

    Integration with Natural Language Processing (NLP). Explore integration with NLP techniques to enhance semantic understanding and processing of legal text changes.

By advancing these research directions, we aim to enable legal information systems that are more accurate, transparent, and capable of meeting the complex demands of legal practitioners, researchers, and AI-driven applications.


Ultimately, the contribution of this paper extends beyond mere data organization. A robust descriptive model, such as the one proposed, provides the precise, versioned textual facts—the ground truth—upon which higher-level legal reasoning rules (e.g., modeled in LKIF-Core) can operate. Without such an accurate and temporally-aware representation, any legal reasoning system would face ambiguity, outdated information, or a lack of verifiable input. Therefore, we frame this model as a foundational prerequisite for effective computational legal reasoning. It provides the essential factual layer for more advanced legal AI systems that perform automated analysis, compliance checking, and logical inference. This positioning elevates its importance, making it a critical component of a holistic legal AI architecture and opening clear pathways for collaborative research between the descriptive modeling and logical reasoning communities to drive interdisciplinary breakthroughs in the field.
