Abstract

arXiv:2411.03284v1 [cs.AI] 5 Nov 2024

While multi-agent systems have been shown
to significantly enhance the performance of
Large Language Models (LLMs) across various tasks and applications, the dense interaction between scaling agents potentially hampers their efficiency and diversity. To address
these challenges, we draw inspiration from
the sparse mixture-of-agents (SMoE) and propose a sparse mixture-of-agents (SMoA) framework to improve the efficiency and diversity
of multi-agent LLMs. Unlike completely connected structures, SMoA introduces novel Response Selection and Early Stopping mechanisms to sparsify information flows among individual LLM agents, striking a balance between performance and efficiency. Additionally, inspired by the expert diversity principle
in SMoE frameworks for workload balance
between experts, we assign distinct role descriptions to each LLM agent, fostering diverse
and divergent thinking. Extensive experiments
on reasoning, alignment, and fairness benchmarks demonstrate that SMoA achieves performance comparable to traditional mixture-ofagents approaches but with significantly lower
computational costs. Further analysis reveals
that SMoA is more stable, has a greater capacity to scale, and offers considerable potential through hyper-parameter optimization.

Introduction

The rapid development of Large Language Models
(LLMs) (Brown et al., 2020; Anil et al., 2023; Gan
et al., 2023; Dubey et al., 2024) in recent years
has significantly advanced in a series of NLP tasks,
such as question answering (Wang et al., 2022;
Tong et al., 2023; Jin et al., 2024a), knowledge discovery (Li et al., 2024a,b; Tan et al., 2024a) and
dialogue systems (Li et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2024).
While these powerful foundation models have been
proven to benefit from extensive training data and

larger model sizes, further scaling up these models is exceptionally costly, often requiring necessitating retraining on several trillion tokens (Zhang
et al., 2024). To overcome this limitation, multiagent LLMs (Liang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024b)
have been explored, enabling LLM systems to incorporate multiple agents, each focused on distinct
objectives and tasks.
The layer-based structure (Liang et al., 2023;
Wang et al., 2024b) is one of the most fundamental strategies for implementing LLM-based
multi-agent systems.
It involves instructing
various LLMs to perform multi-agent debate
(MAD) (Liang et al., 2023; Du et al., 2023) or discussion over multiple rounds to mimic human behavior in problem-solving. While effective in various applications, early layer-based methods (Liang
et al., 2023) process user queries with only one
agent each time, hindering their utilization in
real-world scenarios. Recently, mixture-of-agents
(MoA) (Zhang et al., 2023; Wang et al., 2024b) has
been devised, which uses multiple processors per
layer to handle queries simultaneously for time efficiency purposes. An aggregator then synthesizes
these references to produce a final answer.
Although MoA improves the time efficiency of
multi-agent LLMs, it still faces significant challenges. The first issue is the high token computational cost. While MoA reduces processing time

for user queries, its simultaneous processing nature
increases overall computational demands, limiting
scalability (Qian et al., 2024; Jin et al., 2024b) and
severely undermining the token economy of existing multi-agent systems (Wang et al., 2024a). The
second challenge is the lack of diverse thinking
among LLM agents. Unlike MAD, which explicitly encourages different viewpoints, MoA relies
on agents generating responses based on previous
iterations, leading to homogenization and reduced
performance in tasks requiring divergent thinking.
To overcome the limitations of MoA and enhance the utility of multi-agent LLMs, we propose
Sparse Mixture-of-Agents (SMoA), inspired by the
design of sparse mixture-of-experts (SMoE) (Han
et al., 2015; Shazeer et al., 2016). Unlike fully
connected structures, SMoA introduces sparsity in
agent interactions through two novel agents: the
Judge LLM, which selects high-quality responses
for the next round, and the Moderator LLM, which
controls the information flow and determines when
to end it. This reduces unnecessary data processing, balancing performance with efficiency and enabling better scalability. Moreover, drawing inspiration from the expert diversity principle (Lepikhin
et al.; Jiang et al., 2024a) which is commonly applied in SMoE to balance workload, we assign
distinct role descriptions to each LLM agent, facilitating diverse and divergent thinking in their
problem-solving process.
Previous multi-agent LLMs typically focus on
one or two tasks (Liang et al., 2023; Wang et al.,
2024b), thus failing to obtain a comprehensive evaluation and understanding. In this work, we conduct
extensive experiments across alignment, reasoning, safety, and fairness benchmarks to thoroughly
evaluate various multi-agent LLM strategies. Our
results show that SMoA performs comparably to
MoA with much less computational costs. And
MoA and SMoA are more stable and reliable than
MAD in real-world problem-solving. Further analysis reveals that SMoA is more efficient, promotes
diverse thinking, and has greater potential for scalability compared to MoA.
In summary, our contributions are as follows:
• We identify key limitations in existing multiagent LLM frameworks and propose a novel
SMoA architecture to address them.
• Through extensive experiments across various
tasks, we demonstrate that SMoA achieves performance comparable to MoA while using significantly fewer computational resources.

• We provide further analysis, offering insights into
the comparison of different multi-agent methods
and highlighting the advantages of SMoA.

Related Work

2.1

Multi-agent LLMs

Multi-agent LLMs aim to explore the utilization of
multiple LLMs as agents and direct them to work
collaboratively to solve given problems. One line
of work is layer-based multi-agent methods (Liang
et al., 2023; Du et al., 2023; Chan et al.), in
which multiple LLMs are prompted to debate or
discuss with each other and defend their own response. Additionally, there are some works that
propose mixture-of-agents, to guide various LLMs
to collaborate with each other by referring to others’ responses and generating their own continuation (Zhang et al., 2023; Li et al., 2023b; Wang
et al., 2024b). Based on these two architectures,
some other more complex structures are proposed
to mimic human workflows in various domains,
such as code generation (Hong et al.) and biomedical reasoning (Tang et al.).
2.2

Sparse Mixture-of-Experts

The key idea in SMoE (Shazeer et al., 2016; Chen
et al., 2023; He et al., 2023, 2024) is to activate only
a few experts per input, significantly reducing computational costs compared to the traditional MoE
models. Several improvements have been proposed
to enhance the performance and stability of sparse
MoE models. Routing techniques such as dynamic
routing (Rosenbaum et al., 2018) and Hash-based
routing (Roller et al., 2021) help optimize which
experts are activated, ensuring efficient resource
use. Furthermore, balancing load across experts,
which is first proposed by Fedus et al. (2022), addresses issues like expert imbalance, which can
lead to the underutilization of certain experts and
over-specialization of others. In this work, we borrow insights from SMoE to sparse the information
flow between each layer in SMoA and diversify
agent LLMs using role-playing.

To improve MoA structures and achieve better
performance-efficiency balance, we draw inspiration from the concepts of SMoE. It highlights dynamically selecting only a subset of experts using

• Judge should conduct a comprehensive assessment and comparison to the responses generated
by each processor, filtering out those in low quality and only passing a subset of high-quality and
reasonable responses to the next round.
• Moderator is the agent responsible for controlling the forward process of each processor. It
should make the decision to end the processors’
forward process when the multiple processors
reach a consensus for efficiency consideration.

3.2.2 Early Stopping with Moderator
In the MoA framework, layers of LLMs generate
multiple responses iteratively, passing refined outputs to subsequent layers. This process is controlled by a fixed round number. However, in
some cases, continuing the process across all layers
may not be necessary if a high-quality response is
achieved early on or most of the processors achieve
a consensus. While several previous studies (Liu
et al., 2023) propose rule-based methods to address
it (e.g., stop when the output answers from most
agents are the same), there is still a lack of a general and dynamic approach that can handle various
tasks and applications. To manage this, SMoA introduces a moderator agent M that can dynamically
assess the state of outputs at each layer and decide
whether to stop the process earlier, effectively implementing an early stopping mechanism.

4

Experiment

4.1

Experiment Settings

Benchmarks We conduct evaluation in a wide
range of tasks to measure each method’s alignment level, reasoning capability and safety and
fairness degree. For alignment level evaluation, we
use the Just-Eval (Lin et al., 2023) benchmark,
which contains diverse samples from 5 alignment
datasets: (1) AlpacaEval2 (Li et al., 2023c), (2)
MT-Bench (Zheng et al., 2023), (3) LIMA (Zhou
et al., 2024), (4) HH-RLHF-redteam (Ganguli et al.,
2022) and (5) MaliciousInstruct (Huang et al.). We
follow Lin et al. (2023) and use the GPT-4o to rate
the responses for all the samples. For reasoning, we
use the Massive Multitask Agent Understanding
(MMAU) benchmark (Yin et al., 2024), which involves challenging reasoning tasks including math
understanding, tool-using and code contests. Additionally, we also adopt CEB (Wang et al., 2024c),
a conversation dataset focusing on the examination
of responses’ fairness(e.g., toxicity and stereotype).
Compared Method We compare the following
multi-agent and ensemble methods in experiments:
• Self-Consistency (SC) (Wang et al.) follows a
sample-then-vote pattern and leverages the intuition that a complex reasoning problem typically
admits multiple different ways of thinking leading to its unique correct answer.
• Mixture-of-Agent (MoA) is the fully-connected
multi-agent framework as we introduced in Section 3.1. In our experiment, we follow the implementation of (Wang et al., 2024b).
• Multi-agent Debating (MAD) (Liang et al.,
2023) prompts multiple agents to debate with
each other to encourage diverse and divergent
thinking in the generation process.
• Sparse Mixture-of-Agent (SMoA) is our novelly proposed architecture which incorporates response selection, early stopping and role-playing

4.2

Main Result

Overall Comparison We begin by comparing
the performance of different methods in each
dataset (Table 1, 2 and 3). For Just-Eval, SC, MoA
and SMoA significantly improve the baseline performance, while MAD results in a performance
decline. Notably, SMoA and MoA exhibit comparable results in the alignment task, even when
compared with the powerful GPT-4o model. In the
MMAU dataset, SC stands out with exceptional
performance, considerably boosting the baselines.
MoA and SMoA also provide consistent improve-

ments, whereas MAD fails to deliver positive results. For the CEB dataset, SMoA emerges as the
best for reducing baselines’ toxicity and stereotypes, achieving the best results in three out of the
four baseline LLMs. MoA and SC follow as the
second and third best methods, while MAD again
decreases baseline performance.
In summary: (1) MoA and SMoA offer consistent and comparable improvements across all
three tasks. (2) SC outperforms MoA and SMoA
in reasoning tasks but falls behind in alignment and
fairness. (3) MAD is the least effective method,
leading to performance drops in almost every task.
Comparison of multi-agent methods. To gain
a more fine-grained and deeper understanding of
each multi-agent method, we provide a direct comparison in Figures 3a and 3b with Qwen2-72BInstruct. In Figure 3a, we analyze alignment and
fairness attributes using the Just-Eval and CEB
datasets. Both MoA and SMoA improve the singleagent baseline in terms of depth and engagement,
while also reducing stereotypes and toxicity. Interestingly, we find that although MAD performs
competitively in terms of helpfulness and safety, it
underperforms the baseline in all other aspects. We
attribute this to the binary nature of debate, which
may limit diverse thinking in real-world scenarios.

Conclusion

This paper presents the SMoA architecture, designed to enhance the efficiency and diversity of

multi-agent LLM systems. Our approach leverages
sparsity to optimize the fully connected structure
commonly used in current layer-based multi-agent
methods. Through extensive evaluation across various benchmarks and models, we provide a comprehensive comparison and analysis of existing multiagent approaches. Additionally, further analysis
reveals that SMoA not only improves efficiency
and fosters diverse thinking but also offers greater
scalability potential compared to the original MoA.

Limitation
In this work, we introduce sparsity to the layerbased multi-agent LLMs, which is a fundamental
structure in multi-agent LLM systems. We also
notice there are some network-based multi-agent
structures emerging recently (Zhuge et al.; Qian
et al., 2024). Due to space limitations, we don’t
apply our method to these methods and leave this
for future works. Additionally, while our SMoA
reduces the input token for each processor, all processors are activated for reference generation. One
promising direction for future research is exploring
effective strategies for sparse activation for layerbased multi-agent LLMs.

Ethical Statement
In our study, we follow the ethical considerations
and principles below:
• Transparency: We strive to ensure that our multiagent systems operate transparently, providing
clear explanations of their functionalities, capabilities, and limitations. Users should understand
how these models generate outputs and the potential implications of their use.
• Accountability: We acknowledge our responsibility for the behavior of our LLMs and the
outcomes of their interactions. We commit to
monitoring and addressing any issues that arise
from the deployment of our models, including
unintended consequences or harmful outputs.
• Fairness and Non-Discrimination: We aim to
minimize biases in our multi-agent LLMs, ensuring that they treat all users equitably, regardless
of race, gender, ethnicity, religion, or other characteristics. Our models are designed to promote
inclusivity and diversity in their interactions.
• Privacy and Data Protection: We prioritize the
privacy and security of user data. Our systems do
not store personal information without consent,
and we implement robust measures to protect any

data that may be processed during interactions.
• Safety and Harm Reduction: We are committed
to preventing the dissemination of harmful content and ensuring the safety of users interacting
with our multi-agent systems. Our models are
designed with safety protocols to reduce the risk
of generating harmful or misleading information.
• Collaboration and Community Engagement:
We recognize the importance of collaboration
with stakeholders, including researchers, practitioners, and the communities affected by our
technology. We seek feedback and insights to
continuously improve our models and their ethical deployment.

considering the environmental impact of computational resources and promoting practices that
support sustainability.

More Implement Details

In our main experiment, we construct each multi-agent and ensemble method by using only open-source
models to achieve reproduced and fair comparison. We follow (Wang et al., 2024b) and adopt: Qwen1.572B-Chat (Bai et al., 2023), Qwen2-72B-instruct (Yang et al., 2024), WizardLM-8x22B (Xu et al., 2023),
dbrx-instruct (Team et al., 2024). Besides, we also introduce two more powerful closed-source models
